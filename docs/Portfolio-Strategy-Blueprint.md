# Personal Portfolio — Product & Design Strategy Blueprint
**For: Graphic Designer + UI/UX Designer + UX Researcher**
**Status: Strategy only — no code, no visual design yet**

---

## 01 — Portfolio Positioning

**The core problem:** most designer portfolios read as "here are some pretty pictures." The visitor never learns *how the designer thinks*, so hiring feels risky. A cross-disciplinary designer (Graphic + UI/UX + Research) has a real edge here — but only if the site proves range without looking unfocused.

### Three positioning directions

**A. "The Visual Systems Thinker"**
Positioning: a designer who solves business and communication problems through systems — brand systems, UI systems, research-informed decisions — not one-off pretty assets.
- Best for: startups, agencies, product teams who need someone who can own a problem end-to-end.
- Risk: can feel abstract if not backed by concrete visual proof immediately.

**B. "The Bridge Between Brand and Product"**
Positioning: the rare designer equally fluent in brand/graphic design and digital product (UI/UX), so a company doesn't need to hire two people.
- Best for: startup founders and small agencies who want one hire to cover marketing + product.
- Risk: could read as a generalist/jack-of-all-trades if not anchored by strong flagship work.

**C. "Research-Backed Visual Craft"**
Positioning: leads with UX research rigor, uses graphic design craft as the differentiator that most UX-only portfolios lack.
- Best for: recruiters and product teams evaluating seniority and process maturity.
- Risk: research-first framing can undersell the visual craft that's actually the strongest asset.

### Recommendation: **A hybrid of A and B — "Systems-minded design, from brand to product."**
Lead with the *thinking* (systems, problem-solving, research) but let the *proof* be dual: brand/graphic work and UI/UX work sitting side by side as two expressions of the same design intelligence, not two separate portfolios. This is the differentiator: 95% of portfolios are either "graphic designer" or "UI/UX designer." Being credibly both, presented as one coherent point of view, is rare and valuable.

**First 5 seconds should communicate:** "This is a designer who solves real problems, has strong visual craft, and can be trusted with both a brand and a product." Not "here's a template with nice fonts."

## 02 — Target Personas

### 1. Potential Client (small business / agency client)
- **Goals:** get a brand identity, marketing collateral, or a website that looks credible and converts.
- **Needs:** proof of finished, professional-looking outcomes; clear process; clear pricing/contact path.
- **Questions:** "Has this person done work like mine before? Will they understand my industry? How do I start?"
- **Concerns:** flaky freelancers, scope creep, communication gaps, unclear turnaround.
- **What makes them leave:** no clear way to contact quickly, work that looks like templates, no case studies (only final shots).
- **Decision driver:** relatable past work + an easy, low-friction first contact step (WhatsApp/Fiverr/email).

### 2. Recruiter / Hiring Manager
- **Goals:** quickly assess seniority, process, and role-fit (is this a UI/UX hire, a graphic design hire, or both?).
- **Needs:** scannable case studies with process, not just final screens; clarity on role and tools used; resume/CV access.
- **Questions:** "What was their actual contribution? Can they think, not just execute? Do they fit our team's stage (startup vs enterprise)?"
- **Concerns:** portfolio inflated by tutorials/concept work with no real constraints; unclear ownership on team projects.
- **What makes them leave:** vague case studies with no process, no metrics/outcomes, slow-loading or hard-to-navigate site.
- **Decision driver:** 2–3 strong case studies showing structured thinking (problem → process → outcome) + an About page that reads as credible and specific, not generic.

### 3. Startup Founder
- **Goals:** find someone who can move fast, wear multiple hats (brand + product), and design under ambiguity.
- **Needs:** evidence of end-to-end ownership, comfort with 0-to-1 work, fast communication.
- **Questions:** "Can this person work with incomplete specs? Will they push back with good judgment? Can they do both my landing page and my app UI?"
- **Concerns:** overly "agency-polished" designers who can't move at startup speed; someone who only does pixel work with no strategic input.
- **What makes them leave:** portfolio that only shows polished final shots with no story of ambiguity/tradeoffs.
- **Decision driver:** range (brand + product in one place) and a tone that feels like a collaborator, not a vendor.

### 4. Creative Agency / Creative Director
- **Goals:** assess craft level and versatility for potential freelance/contract collaboration.
- **Needs:** high visual craft bar, evidence of typography/composition skill, consistent quality across pieces.
- **Questions:** "Is the craft genuinely strong, or is it AI-template-level? Can they take direction and still bring a POV?"
- **Concerns:** portfolios that look like every other portfolio (same layout kits, same stock treatments).
- **What makes them leave:** generic visual identity, inconsistent quality between projects, lack of a distinct point of view.
- **Decision driver:** visual distinctiveness + a coherent personal design language across the whole site (the site itself is a portfolio piece).

## 03 — Ideal User Journey

**Landing → Understanding → Exploring Work → Evaluating Skill → Reading Case Study → Trust → Contact → Hiring**

| Stage | User Goal | UX Friction to Avoid | Trust-Building Moment | CTA Opportunity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Landing | "What is this, is it worth my time?" | Slow load, unclear hero message | Confident, specific hero statement | Scroll cue, "See work" |
| Understanding | "What do they actually do?" | Buzzword soup, no scope clarity | Clear service/skill framing | "View selected work" |
| Exploring Work | "Is their work good and relevant to me?" | Overwhelming grid, unclear categories | Curated, high-quality thumbnails | Click into project |
| Evaluating Skill | "Do they understand problems, or just decorate?" | Only final screens, no process | Visible process artifacts (research, iterations) | Continue reading |
| Reading Case Study | "Can I trust their thinking?" | Wall of text, no narrative structure | Clear problem → decision → outcome arc | "See more work" / "Get in touch" |
| Trust | "Are they legit and reachable?" | No social proof, no real bio | Testimonials, real photo, specific bio details | About page link |
| Contact | "How fast can I reach them?" | Contact buried, form-only, no directness | Multiple fast contact channels | WhatsApp / Email / Form |
| Hiring | "Do I feel confident sending the first message?" | Any prior friction compounds here | Consistent professionalism throughout | Final CTA reinforcement |

**Drop-off risks:** slow-loading image-heavy grids, case studies that are just image dumps, no visible way to contact without scrolling to the very bottom, and inconsistent quality between the "top" projects and the rest of the grid.

## 04 — Information Architecture

### Necessary pages (lean, not bloated)
1. **Home** — positioning, hero, featured work, quick proof, primary CTA
2. **Work** (single unified page, not split by category) — filterable grid of all projects across Graphic Design + Branding + UI/UX
3. **Case Study** (dynamic template, one per project) — deep dive
4. **About** — story, process, philosophy, tools, resume download
5. **Contact** — direct channels + short form

### Pages to avoid creating separately
- Do **not** create separate top-level pages for "Graphic Design," "Branding," "UI/UX" — this fragments a small portfolio (likely 8–15 projects total) into thin pages. Instead, use **filters/tags on one Work page**. Separate pages only make sense past ~25+ projects per category.
- No separate "Services" page initially — fold service framing into Home + About, add later only if pursuing more client (vs. recruiter) traffic.
- No public "Blog" unless content is already being produced consistently (Joy already runs @joyuidesigns content — if that pipeline is active, a lightweight Journal/Insights page *could* be a later-phase addition, not launch-critical).

### Navigation structure
- **Primary nav:** Home · Work · About · Contact (4 items max — resist the urge to add more)
- **Secondary nav (within Work page):** filter chips — All / UI-UX / Branding / Graphic Design / Case Studies
- **Footer nav:** quick links (Work, About, Contact), social/contact icons (Behance, LinkedIn, Fiverr, Email, WhatsApp), copyright

### Case study structure (see Section 07 for full detail)
Each case study lives at its own URL (`/work/[project-slug]`) but shares one consistent template across categories, with conditional sections depending on project type (UI/UX vs. branding).

## 05 — Homepage Strategy

### Recommended section hierarchy

**1. Hero**
- Purpose: instantly establish who Joy is and what makes him different.
- Question answered: "Who is this and why should I care?"
- Content: name, positioning statement, 1-line differentiator, primary CTA, subtle visual proof (a hint of work, not full case studies).
- UX goal: clarity + confidence in under 5 seconds.
- CTA: "View Selected Work" (primary), "Let's Talk" (secondary, subtle).

**2. Quick Proof / Trust Strip**
- Purpose: reduce early skepticism.
- Content: years of experience, client/brand logos (if permissible) or a short credibility line (e.g., "4+ years · Digital Hub BD · Freelance clients across [industries]").
- UX goal: fast credibility without needing a full About read.

**3. Featured Work (curated, not all work)**
- Purpose: show 3–4 best projects, mixing graphic + UI/UX, to prove range immediately.
- Content: large, high-quality project cards with a one-line outcome/insight per project (not just a title).
- CTA: "View all work."

**4. Approach / How I Think**
- Purpose: differentiate from "just an executor" — show process maturity.
- Content: a short 3–4 step framework (e.g., Understand → Define → Design → Refine) presented visually, not as a wall of text.
- UX goal: plant the idea that hiring Joy means hiring judgment, not just output.

**5. About Snapshot**
- Purpose: humanize, build trust.
- Content: short bio, photo, 2–3 specific facts (background, current role, focus areas), link to full About.

**6. Testimonials / Social Proof** *(if available)*
- Purpose: third-party validation, especially important for the Client and Founder personas.
- Content: 2–3 short quotes with name/role.

**7. Final CTA / Contact Band**
- Purpose: convert warmed-up visitors immediately, no scrolling hunt.
- Content: direct restatement of availability + fastest contact channel.
- CTA: "Start a project" / WhatsApp or Email direct link.

### Hero concepts (3 explored)

**Concept 1 — Statement-Led Hero**
Large, confident typographic statement (e.g., "I design brands and products that earn trust.") with minimal supporting visual. Typography *is* the visual proof of graphic design skill.

**Concept 2 — Split Proof Hero**
Headline + a tight visual composition showing 2–3 fragments of real work (one brand piece, one UI screen) arranged as a graphic composition — proves range immediately, not just claims it.

**Concept 3 — Narrative Hero**
Headline + a single strong sentence framing Joy as a problem-solver ("Graphic design and UI/UX, used to solve real business problems — not just decorate them.") with a scroll-triggered reveal of work below.

### Recommendation: **Concept 2 — Split Proof Hero.**
It's the only concept that *shows* the cross-disciplinary positioning instead of just claiming it, satisfying Section 01's differentiator immediately, while still leaving room for strong typography (satisfying the "editorial, strong typography" visual direction).

## 06 — Portfolio / Work Strategy

- **Structure:** one unified Work page, not split by discipline. This reflects the "systems thinker" positioning — the categories are lenses, not silos.
- **Featured projects:** 3–4 flagship pieces pinned to the top regardless of filter state, manually curated (not auto-sorted by date).
- **Filtering:** yes, but lightweight — a single row of filter chips (All / UI-UX / Branding / Graphic Design), not a complex faceted filter system. With a realistic project count (likely under ~20 at launch), heavy filtering/search is unnecessary complexity and should be avoided per the "don't create pages/features just because they're common" instruction.
- **Project cards:** large image, project title, 1-line outcome/insight (not just category tag), category tag as a small label.
- **Discovery experience recommendation:** curated-first (featured work up top) + simple filtered grid below. This balances "quick scan" (recruiters) with "browse everything" (creative directors).

## 07 — Case Study UX

### UI/UX project template
**Problem → Research → Insights → Strategy → IA → Wireframes → UI → Testing → Outcome**

Each stage should be 2–4 sentences of *narrated decision-making*, not a caption under a screenshot. The goal: show *why* a decision was made, not just *what* was designed. Include at least one artifact per stage that isn't a polished final screen (a sketch, a flow diagram, a research note) — this is what separates senior-level case studies from "final shots with a story bolted on."

### Branding project template
**Context → Research → Strategy → Moodboard → Concept → Logo → Typography → Color → Identity → Applications → Outcome**

Same principle: show the *narrowing* process (multiple directions explored, why one won) rather than presenting the final logo as if it appeared fully formed.

### How to demonstrate senior-level thinking (not just show images)
1. **State the constraint** up front (budget, timeline, stakeholder, technical limitation) — constraints are what separate junior "made it pretty" work from senior "solved it despite X" work.
2. **Show one rejected direction** briefly, with the reason it was rejected — this proves judgment.
3. **Tie every major decision to a "why."** Never caption an image with just what it is.
4. **End with an honest outcome**, even if it's process-based (e.g., "delivered on time, client adopted system across 3 channels") rather than fabricated metrics if hard numbers aren't available.
5. **Keep text tight.** 40–60 words per section max — this is a portfolio, not a report; density of insight matters more than volume.

## 08 — Graphic Design Showcase

Recommended presentation system:
- **Grouped by project, not by asset type** — a branding project shows logo + collateral + social + print together as one story, not scattered across separate galleries.
- **Context shots over flat asset dumps** — mockups showing real-world application (packaging in hand, poster on a wall, social post in a phone frame) build more credibility than flat PNG exports, but should be used selectively, not on every single asset.
- **Consistent presentation frame/template** across all graphic design projects (same mockup style, same grid) so the *portfolio itself* looks systemized — reinforcing the "systems thinker" positioning.
- **Categories to represent:** branding/logo, editorial/brochure, social/campaign, packaging, print — but only include categories where the work is genuinely strong; a thin, weak category actively hurts more than an absent one.

---

## 09 — UI/UX Showcase

- Present **flows and structure, not just polished screens**: include at least one user flow diagram or IA sketch per UI/UX case study.
- Show **before/after or iteration states** where possible — even 2 versions of a screen with a one-line "why it changed" note does more UX-credibility work than 5 final polished screens.
- **Design systems:** if a component/design system was built (Joy has done this — buttons, input fields, variables), show it explicitly as a small showcase within the case study — this specifically appeals to the recruiter and agency personas evaluating systems-thinking maturity.
- **Communicate UX thinking, not just visuals**, by always pairing a screen with a one-line rationale: what user problem this specific screen solves, not just what it looks like.

## 10 — Contact / Conversion Strategy

### Recommended contact stack
- **Primary CTA:** Email (professional, universally trusted, works for all four personas) — displayed as a direct `mailto:` link, not hidden behind a form.
- **Secondary CTA:** WhatsApp (fastest for Bangladesh-based clients and freelance leads) — direct `wa.me` link.
- **Tertiary / passive:** LinkedIn (recruiters), Behance (creative directors/agencies), Fiverr (clients seeking a transactional engagement).
- **Contact form:** short, optional — name, email, project type (dropdown), message. Never make the form the *only* path; it should sit alongside the direct links, not replace them.

### Where CTAs should appear
- Hero (secondary CTA)
- End of every case study
- Homepage final contact band
- Sticky/persistent element in nav or footer (e.g., a small "Let's talk" button always visible) — low-friction, not intrusive

### Contact page structure
- Short reinforcing headline ("Let's build something.")
- Direct contact methods listed clearly with icons
- Short form beneath, for those who prefer it
- Optional: expected response time ("Usually replies within 24 hours") — reduces anxiety-driven drop-off

### Friction reduction
- No login/signup required anywhere.
- No multi-step contact wizard.
- Keep the form to 3–4 fields max.

## 11 — Visual Direction

### Direction A — "Editorial Minimal"
- **Mood:** confident, quiet, magazine-like.
- **Typography:** a strong serif or high-contrast sans for headlines, neutral grotesk for body.
- **Color usage:** off-white base, deep blue for text/structure, orange used sparingly as a single accent (CTA buttons, small highlights only).
- **Grid:** strict editorial column grid, generous margins.
- **Image treatment:** large, uncropped, full-bleed occasionally for impact.
- **Card style:** borderless, separated by whitespace rather than boxes/shadows.
- **Motion:** minimal — fade/slide on scroll only, no decorative animation.
- **Personality:** premium, restrained, confident.

### Direction B — "Structured Studio"
- **Mood:** systemized, professional, agency-like.
- **Typography:** geometric sans throughout, tight tracking on headlines.
- **Color usage:** off-white base, deep blue as dominant structural color (headers, nav), orange as functional accent (links, active states).
- **Grid:** visible grid lines/structure as a design motif (subtle rules, numbered sections).
- **Image treatment:** consistent aspect ratios, uniform framing across all project cards.
- **Card style:** light 1px borders, no heavy shadows, sharp corners (minimal/no border-radius).
- **Motion:** subtle hover states, structured reveal animations tied to grid.
- **Personality:** precise, systems-minded — directly reinforces the "Systems Thinker" positioning.

### Direction C — "Bold Graphic"
- **Mood:** expressive, design-forward, portfolio-as-artwork.
- **Typography:** oversized display type as a graphic element itself, mixed weights.
- **Color usage:** deep blue and orange used in bolder blocks/shapes, off-white as negative space.
- **Grid:** asymmetric layout, intentional grid-breaking in select moments.
- **Image treatment:** cropped/composed images used as design elements, not just content.
- **Card style:** graphic shapes as card backgrounds/dividers.
- **Motion:** slightly more expressive transitions, but still controlled.
- **Personality:** memorable, distinctly "designer-made" — highest risk of feeling busy if not executed with restraint.

### Recommendation: **Direction B — "Structured Studio,"** with typographic confidence borrowed from Direction A.
This most directly reinforces the recommended positioning (systems thinker, bridge between brand and product) while satisfying every stated constraint: white/off-white, deep blue, orange accent, premium, minimal, editorial, strong typography, generous whitespace, and explicitly avoiding SaaS/glassmorphism/excessive-rounding/dark-mode defaults.

*Note: this palette (deep blue / orange / off-white) differs from Joy's existing personal brand identity (navy #0F1F3D / gold #C9943A, Syne Bold) — worth a deliberate decision on whether the portfolio should extend the existing brand or intentionally diverge for this specific site. Flagging this now rather than assuming.*

## 12 — Design System Strategy

- **Color tokens:** `background` (off-white), `background-alt` (subtle warm-white for section breaks), `text-primary` (deep blue, near-black), `text-secondary` (muted blue-gray), `accent` (orange — CTAs/links/highlights only), `border` (light neutral).
- **Typography scale:** modular scale (e.g., 1.25 ratio) — Display / H1 / H2 / H3 / Body-Large / Body / Caption. One display/headline typeface + one workhorse body typeface — no more than 2 font families.
- **Spacing system:** 8px base unit scale (8/16/24/32/48/64/96/128) for consistent rhythm.
- **Grid:** 12-column desktop grid, generous gutters; container max-width tuned for editorial reading line-length (not full-bleed text).
- **Container widths:** narrow (text/case-study copy), wide (project grids/hero).
- **Border radius:** minimal to none — sharp/near-sharp corners to reinforce "structured" personality, avoiding the generic rounded-SaaS look explicitly ruled out.
- **Shadows:** avoid heavy drop shadows; use borders/whitespace for separation instead.
- **Buttons:** primary (solid deep blue or orange, sharp corners), secondary (outline/text-link style).
- **Cards:** project card, case-study-section card, testimonial card — consistent padding and image-aspect-ratio rules.
- **Navigation:** minimal top nav, persistent but unobtrusive; mobile: simple slide-in or full-screen menu.
- **Tags:** small, uppercase, letter-spaced labels for project categories — functions as a design element, not just metadata.
- **Forms:** underline-style or minimal-border inputs, consistent with the editorial aesthetic (avoid boxy SaaS-style form fields).
- **Project components:** project card, project grid, filter chip.
- **Case study components:** section header, process step block, image-with-caption, pull-quote, outcome summary block.
- **Responsive rules:** type scale steps down at defined breakpoints; grid drops from 12 → 6 → 4 columns at tablet/mobile.

## 13 — Responsive UX Strategy

Not a simple scale-down — each breakpoint should be treated as its own design decision:

- **Navigation:** desktop shows full horizontal nav; mobile collapses to a minimal menu icon with a full-screen overlay (not a cramped dropdown).
- **Hero:** desktop can use the Split Proof composition (headline + visual fragments side-by-side); mobile stacks vertically with the headline first and visual proof condensed to a single strong image, not a cramped composite.
- **Typography:** desktop can support larger display sizes and longer line lengths; mobile needs a distinct (not just scaled) type scale — tighter headline sizes, shorter line lengths for readability.
- **Project grid:** desktop 3-column, tablet 2-column, mobile single column, full-width cards — never shrink a 3-column grid down to tiny mobile cards.
- **Case study layout:** desktop can use side-by-side text/image sections; mobile stacks strictly sequentially with image-first, text-second per section for scannability.
- **Images:** serve responsive/optimized sizes per breakpoint; avoid loading desktop-resolution images on mobile.
- **CTA:** desktop CTAs can be inline; mobile benefits from a persistent bottom contact bar/button for the highest-intent action (contact), since thumb-reach and scroll fatigue are real constraints.
- **Footer:** desktop multi-column; mobile stacks into a single accordion-free simple list — keep it short so it doesn't become a scroll burden.
- **Contact:** mobile should prioritize tap-to-call/WhatsApp/email links above the form, since typing a form on mobile is higher friction.
- **Admin dashboard:** desktop-first tool (content management is not a mobile use case) — mobile view can be a simplified read-only/quick-edit view at most; don't over-invest in mobile admin UX.

## 14 — Admin Dashboard Strategy

### Minimum viable CMS scope
- **Dashboard:** overview — total projects, published vs. draft count, recent activity.
- **Projects:** list view (title, category, status, featured flag, last updated), create/edit/delete.
- **Categories:** manage the fixed set (UI/UX, Branding, Graphic Design) — likely doesn't need to be infinitely extensible; a small fixed taxonomy is fine and simpler.
- **Case Studies:** structured editor per project (see below) rather than a single freeform text field — this keeps case study formatting consistent site-wide without needing custom code per project.
- **Media:** simple upload/manage library, reused across projects.
- **Draft/Published state:** every project needs this — lets Joy build a project incrementally without it going live prematurely.
- **Featured projects:** boolean flag + manual ordering control for the homepage's featured section.

### Project editor (content model)
```
Project {
  title
  slug
  category (UI/UX | Branding | Graphic Design)
  tags[]
  status (draft | published)
  featured (boolean)
  featuredOrder (number)
  coverImage
  summary (1-line, used on cards)
  client (optional)
  role
  year
  caseStudy: {
    problem
    research
    insights
    strategy
    process[] (repeatable block: title + description + image)
    outcome
  }
}
```

This structured model is what enables *consistent* case study presentation across the site (Section 07) without hand-coding each one — a real long-term time-saver given Joy's pattern of producing many structured content pieces already (per his history with structured PDF/design-system work).

## 15 — Technical Product Requirements

- **Framework:** Next.js (App Router) + TypeScript — supports SSG/ISR for fast-loading, SEO-friendly project pages, which matters a lot for an image-heavy portfolio.
- **Styling:** Tailwind CSS, mapped directly to the design tokens defined in Section 12 (avoids ad hoc values, keeps system consistent).
- **CMS/database:** a lightweight headless approach — either a simple database (e.g., Postgres via a managed provider) with a custom minimal admin UI, or a headless CMS (e.g., Sanity/Payload) if Joy wants a faster-to-build editor without hand-building the admin dashboard from scratch. Given the modest content volume (a personal portfolio, not a large CMS), a headless CMS is likely the pragmatic choice over building custom auth + database + admin UI from zero.
- **Authentication:** only needed for the admin area — a single-user auth flow (email/password or magic link) is sufficient; no need for multi-role complexity.
- **Image storage/optimization:** a CDN-backed image service (e.g., Cloudinary, or the CMS's built-in asset pipeline) + Next.js Image component for responsive delivery — critical given the image-heavy nature of the site.
- **SEO:** per-project meta tags/OG images (important — case studies get shared directly, e.g., on LinkedIn), sitemap, semantic HTML structure.
- **Performance:** lazy-load below-the-fold project images, optimize hero assets aggressively (first-impression speed matters most), target sub-2s LCP.
- **Accessibility:** proper heading hierarchy, alt text fields built into the CMS content model (not an afterthought), sufficient color contrast (deep blue on off-white passes easily; verify orange-on-white for any text use).
- **Analytics:** a lightweight, privacy-respecting analytics tool (e.g., Plausible/Vercel Analytics) to track which projects/case studies get the most engagement — useful signal for future portfolio curation.
- **Responsive architecture:** mobile-first Tailwind breakpoints, tested against the specific rules in Section 13, not just default scaling.

**Recommendation:** Next.js + TypeScript + Tailwind + a headless CMS (fastest path to a real admin experience without overbuilding custom infrastructure) + a CDN image pipeline. Powerful enough for a professional, scalable portfolio; not overengineered for what is fundamentally a content site with a small, well-defined content model.

## 16 — Figma Strategy

### What to build in Figma before code
1. **Design system file:** color styles, type styles, spacing/effect variables — mirroring Section 12 exactly, using Figma **variables** (not just styles) so light/structural theming stays consistent and exportable as design tokens later.
2. **Component library:** buttons, nav, project card, case-study blocks, tags, form fields — built as proper components with variants (default/hover/active states), not one-off shapes.
3. **Frames per breakpoint:** Desktop (1440), Tablet (768–1024), Mobile (375–430) — for Home, Work, one full Case Study template, About, Contact. Not every project needs a full custom Figma design — only the *template*, since projects are content-driven, not uniquely designed per page.
4. **Admin dashboard:** low-fidelity/mid-fidelity only — this is an internal tool, doesn't need the same visual polish investment as the public-facing site.

### Keeping Figma and code consistent
- Treat the Section 12 design tokens as the **single source of truth** — define them once, mirror identically as Figma variables and Tailwind theme config, so neither drifts from the other.
- Build Figma components 1:1 with the eventual coded components (same naming: `ProjectCard`, `CaseStudySection`, etc.) so implementation is a direct translation, not a re-interpretation.
- Any change to spacing/color/type in Figma should be a variable update, not a manual per-frame edit — this is what actually keeps a growing file consistent over time.

## 17 — Competitive / Inspiration Principles

Patterns that separate strong designer portfolios from average ones (principles, not visual trends to copy):

- **Restraint over density.** The strongest portfolios show fewer, better projects rather than everything ever made. Curation itself signals seniority.
- **Process visibility builds more trust than polish alone.** Recruiters and creative directors specifically look for evidence of thinking, not just output.
- **The site itself is treated as a design artifact.** Every strong portfolio's own UX/visual quality is implicitly the largest case study on the site — inconsistency here undermines everything else.
- **Specificity over generic claims.** "I design brands and products" (generic) reads weaker than "I help startups turn early ideas into brand systems and usable products" (specific value + audience).
- **Fast, obvious contact paths.** The best-converting portfolios never make contacting the designer feel like an investigation.
- **Consistent presentation templates across projects** (not a uniquely designed page per project) actually reads as *more* professional — it signals a systemized process rather than one-off improvisation.

## 18 — UX Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Too much content / too many projects diluting quality | Curate hard: launch with 8–12 strongest projects max; quality bar over quantity |
| Weak/vague positioning | Lock the positioning statement (Section 01) before any design work begins; test it on someone unfamiliar with Joy's work |
| Confusing navigation | Keep primary nav to 4 items; no nested dropdown menus |
| Too many categories fragmenting the work | Single Work page with lightweight filters, not separate category pages (Section 04) |
| Weak case studies (image dumps, no narrative) | Enforce the structured content model (Section 14) — every case study must fill Problem/Process/Outcome fields, no exceptions |
| Slow loading (image-heavy site) | CDN + responsive images + lazy loading (Section 15); test performance before launch, not after |
| Poor mobile experience | Design mobile breakpoints deliberately (Section 13), not as an afterthought scale-down |
| Excessive animation undermining "premium minimal" positioning | Motion limited to subtle scroll/hover states only; no decorative animation, per stated constraints |
| Difficult/buried contact flow | Contact CTA present in hero, case study ends, and persistent nav/footer (Section 10) |
| Generic visual identity (looks like a template) | Direction B with typographic confidence (Section 11), sharp/no-radius system to explicitly avoid the "AI-template" and "SaaS" look the brief flags |
| Lack of trust signals | Testimonials if available; specific, non-generic About page; real, specific project outcomes rather than vague claims |
| Inconsistent quality across projects diluting strong ones | Curation discipline — weak projects should be left out entirely, not included "for volume" |

## FINAL DELIVERABLE SUMMARY

1. **Positioning:** "Systems-minded design, from brand to product" — a designer who solves problems with both graphic/brand craft and UI/UX rigor, proven side by side rather than siloed.
2. **Target audience:** Primary — startup founders and recruiters/hiring managers; secondary — small business clients and creative agencies/directors.
3. **User journey:** Landing → Understanding → Exploring Work → Evaluating Skill → Case Study → Trust → Contact → Hiring, with trust-building process artifacts at every stage and low-friction contact throughout.
4. **Information architecture:** Home · Work (unified, filterable) · Case Study (dynamic template) · About · Contact — no fragmented category pages.
5. **Homepage structure:** Hero → Quick Proof → Featured Work → Approach → About Snapshot → Testimonials → Final Contact Band.
6. **Hero direction:** Split Proof Hero — headline + real composed work fragments proving range immediately.
7. **Portfolio structure:** unified grid, curated featured section, lightweight category filters, no heavy search/facets.
8. **Case study structure:** structured Problem→Process→Outcome model (UI/UX and Branding variants), enforced via CMS content model, always showing at least one non-final-polish artifact per project.
9. **Visual direction:** Direction B — Structured Studio (with Direction A's typographic confidence) — off-white, deep blue, orange accent, sharp corners, minimal shadows, editorial grid.
10. **Design system:** token-based (color/type/spacing/radius/shadow), 8px spacing scale, 12-column grid, two-typeface system, consistent component library.
11. **Responsive strategy:** deliberate per-breakpoint redesign (not scaling), mobile-first contact prioritization, persistent mobile contact bar.
12. **Admin architecture:** structured project + case-study content model, draft/published states, featured/ordering controls, single-user auth.
13. **Technical architecture:** Next.js + TypeScript + Tailwind + headless CMS + CDN image pipeline + lightweight analytics.
14. **Figma strategy:** token-driven design system file mirrored 1:1 with code variables/components, breakpoint frames for templates only (not every project).
15. **UX risks:** addressed per Section 18 — chiefly curation discipline, structured case-study enforcement, and contact-friction reduction.

## PRIORITIZED ROADMAP

**PHASE 01 — Research & Strategy** *(this document)*
Positioning lock, persona validation, competitive principle extraction.

**PHASE 02 — UX Architecture**
Finalize IA, sitemap, user flows, wireframe the case study content model and admin content model.

**PHASE 03 — Design System**
Define tokens (color, type, spacing, radius) in Figma variables; build core component set (buttons, cards, nav, tags, form fields).

**PHASE 04 — Visual Design**
Design Home, Work, one full Case Study template, About, Contact across desktop/tablet/mobile frames in Figma, using Direction B.

**PHASE 05 — Development**
Build Next.js + Tailwind implementation matching Figma components 1:1; wire up CMS content model; implement responsive rules per Section 13.

**PHASE 06 — Admin/CMS**
Build/configure the project and case-study editor, draft/publish flow, featured ordering, media management, single-user auth.

**PHASE 07 — Responsive Optimization**
QA and refine across real breakpoints — not just resizing a browser, but validating the deliberate mobile redesign decisions from Section 13.

**PHASE 08 — UX/UI Audit**
Full pass against Section 18's risk list; test the site cold with someone unfamiliar with the work; verify performance (load speed), accessibility, and contact-flow friction.

**PHASE 09 — Final Polish**
Micro-interactions/motion pass (kept minimal per constraints), copy tightening, final performance pass, launch readiness check (SEO meta, OG images, analytics wired up).
